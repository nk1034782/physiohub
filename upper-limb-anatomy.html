01

Overview & Anatomical Position

Study principle: Learn the upper limb from proximal to distal: shoulder girdle → arm → forearm → wrist → hand. For each structure, connect anatomy with movement and function.

Regions

The upper limb can be described as the shoulder/pectoral girdle, arm, forearm and hand. The arm is between shoulder and elbow; the forearm is between elbow and wrist.

Proximal-to-Distal Logic

Start with bones and landmarks, then joints, muscles, nerves and vessels. Finally relate each structure to movement and clinical examination.

Key Terms

Anterior/posterior, medial/lateral, proximal/distal, superficial/deep, flexion/extension, abduction/adduction and rotation are essential terms.

02

Osteology — Bones & Landmarks

🦴

Clavicle

An S-shaped bone connecting the upper limb to the axial skeleton. It has medial (sternal) and lateral (acromial) ends and helps position the shoulder.

🦴

Scapula

A triangular posterior thoracic bone. Important landmarks include spine, acromion, coracoid process, glenoid cavity, supraspinous and infraspinous fossae.

🦴

Humerus

The long bone of the arm. Proximal landmarks include head, surgical neck, greater and lesser tubercles; distal landmarks include capitulum, trochlea and epicondyles.

🦴

Radius

Lateral forearm bone in anatomical position. Its head articulates with the capitulum and ulna; the distal radius contributes importantly to the wrist joint.

🦴

Ulna

Medial forearm bone. Olecranon and coronoid processes form major proximal elbow landmarks; the distal ulna participates in the distal radioulnar region.

✋

Hand Bones

Eight carpals form the wrist, followed by five metacarpals and phalanges. The thumb has two phalanges; the other digits usually have three.

03

Joints of the Upper Limb

Sternoclavicular Joint

A synovial saddle-type joint with important movement between the clavicle and axial skeleton. An articular disc contributes to congruence and load transfer.

Acromioclavicular Joint

A plane synovial joint between acromion and clavicle. Small gliding movements are important for scapular motion.

Shoulder (Glenohumeral)

A synovial ball-and-socket joint designed for large mobility. Stability depends on the labrum, capsule, ligaments and surrounding muscles.

Elbow Joint

Primarily a hinge joint involving humerus with ulna and radius. Flexion and extension are its principal movements.

Radioulnar Joints

Proximal and distal radioulnar joints work together to permit pronation and supination.

Wrist & Hand

The radiocarpal and intercarpal joints allow wrist motion; carpometacarpal, metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints provide hand function.

Shoulder stability: The glenohumeral joint sacrifices some bony stability for mobility. The rotator cuff and other soft tissues help centre the humeral head during movement.
04

Muscles — Attachments, Actions & Nerve Supply

Deltoid

Origin: clavicle, acromion and scapular spine. Insertion: deltoid tuberosity. Action: arm abduction; anterior/posterior fibres assist flexion/extension and rotation. Nerve: axillary.

Supraspinatus

From supraspinous fossa to greater tubercle. Initiates shoulder abduction and contributes to glenohumeral stability. Supplied by the suprascapular nerve.

Infraspinatus

From infraspinous fossa to greater tubercle. Major external rotator of the shoulder. Supplied by the suprascapular nerve.

Teres Minor

From lateral scapular border to greater tubercle. External rotation and stabilization of the shoulder. Axillary nerve.

Subscapularis

From subscapular fossa to lesser tubercle. Internal rotation and stabilization of the humeral head. Upper and lower subscapular nerves.

Biceps Brachii

Long and short heads arise from scapula and insert on radius. Flexes elbow and supinates forearm; also assists shoulder flexion. Musculocutaneous nerve.

Triceps Brachii

Three heads; inserts on olecranon. Primary elbow extensor. Radial nerve.

Brachialis

Anterior arm muscle inserting on ulna. A powerful elbow flexor. Musculocutaneous nerve is the major supply.

Pronator Teres

Crosses from the medial epicondyle/coronoid region toward the lateral radius. Pronates forearm and assists elbow flexion. Median nerve.

Flexor Carpi Radialis

Anterior forearm muscle inserting toward bases of the second and third metacarpals. Wrist flexion with radial deviation. Median nerve.

Extensor Carpi Radialis Longus

Extends and abducts the wrist; important in wrist positioning. Radial nerve.

Extensor Digitorum

Extends the fingers and assists wrist extension. Posterior interosseous nerve.

Rotator cuff mnemonic: SITS = Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres minor, Subscapularis.
05

Nerves & Brachial Plexus

Brachial Plexus

Classically organized as roots → trunks → divisions → cords → terminal branches. It provides motor and sensory innervation to much of the upper limb.

Musculocutaneous Nerve

Supplies the anterior arm compartment and continues as the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm. Important for elbow flexion.

Axillary Nerve

Supplies deltoid and teres minor and sensation over the lateral shoulder region. Vulnerable around the surgical neck of the humerus.

Radial Nerve

Supplies much of the extensor musculature and provides sensation to parts of the posterior/lateral limb. Clinically important around the humeral shaft.

Median Nerve

Important motor and sensory nerve of the forearm and hand. It passes through the carpal tunnel with the flexor tendons.

Ulnar Nerve

Supplies many intrinsic hand muscles and contributes to forearm muscles. It passes posterior to the medial epicondyle at the elbow.

06

Blood Supply

Subclavian → Axillary

The subclavian artery becomes the axillary artery at the lateral border of the first rib. The axillary artery supplies the shoulder and upper limb through multiple branches.

Brachial Artery

Continuation of the axillary artery in the arm. It divides near the elbow region into radial and ulnar arteries.

Radial & Ulnar Arteries

These arteries supply the forearm and hand and contribute to superficial and deep palmar arterial networks.

Venous Return

Superficial veins such as cephalic and basilic veins contribute to upper-limb venous drainage; deep veins accompany arteries.

07

Regional Anatomy

Axilla

A pyramidal space between upper limb and thorax containing major vessels, nerves and lymphatic structures.

Arm Compartments

The anterior compartment is mainly associated with elbow flexion; the posterior compartment is mainly associated with elbow extension.

Cubital Fossa

A clinically important triangular depression anterior to the elbow containing major neurovascular structures.

Carpal Tunnel

A passage at the wrist containing the median nerve and long flexor tendons. Increased pressure can affect the median nerve.

Anatomical Snuffbox

A depression on the lateral wrist made prominent with thumb extension; a useful surface-anatomy landmark.

Scapular Region

The scapula and surrounding muscles coordinate shoulder motion. Scapular positioning influences glenohumeral mechanics.

09

Clinical Anatomy for Physiotherapy

Shoulder Pain

Painful shoulder movement may involve multiple tissues and movement factors. Assessment should consider history, range, strength and functional limitations rather than assuming one structure.

Rotator Cuff Problems

The cuff contributes to humeral-head control and shoulder movement. Clinical assessment commonly considers pain, strength and functional movement.

Lateral Elbow Pain

Lateral elbow pain is commonly associated with the common extensor tendon region and load-related symptoms. Assessment should be based on the clinical presentation.

Carpal Tunnel Syndrome

Median nerve compression at the wrist can cause sensory symptoms and weakness in median-innervated functions. Clinical examination should guide management.

Nerve Injury Patterns

Knowing nerve territories helps relate weakness and sensory changes to possible lesion sites, while recognizing that clinical diagnosis requires full assessment.

Fracture Relevance

The surgical neck of humerus, elbow region and wrist are important areas where fractures can have neurovascular implications.

Important: This page is for education and revision. Clinical diagnosis and treatment decisions require appropriate examination, professional training and current clinical guidance.
10

High-Yield Revision Notes

Remember SITS

Supraspinatus • Infraspinatus • Teres minor • Subscapularis.

Important Nerves

Focus strongly on radial, median and ulnar nerves, while also learning axillary and musculocutaneous nerves.

Bone Sequence

Clavicle/scapula → humerus → radius/ulna → carpals → metacarpals → phalanges.

Study Every Muscle

Use: origin → insertion → action → nerve supply → functional movement.

Study Every Joint

Identify joint type → surfaces → ligaments/capsule → movements → stabilizers → clinical relevance.

Physio Connection

Connect anatomy to posture, range of motion, muscle strength, nerve function and functional activities.
