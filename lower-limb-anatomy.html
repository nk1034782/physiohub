01

Overview & Anatomical Position

Study principle: Learn the lower limb from proximal to distal: pelvis → thigh → knee → leg → ankle → foot. For every structure, connect anatomy with movement, weight-bearing and function.

Regions

The lower limb includes the pelvic girdle and free lower limb. The free limb can be studied as thigh, leg and foot. The leg specifically refers to the region between knee and ankle.

Weight Bearing

Compared with the upper limb, the lower limb is strongly adapted for support, balance, locomotion and efficient transfer of body weight.

Key Terms

Anterior/posterior, medial/lateral, proximal/distal, flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, internal/external rotation, dorsiflexion and plantarflexion.

02

Osteology — Bones & Landmarks

🦴

Hip Bone

The hip bone is formed by the fused ilium, ischium and pubis. Important landmarks include iliac crest, ASIS, AIIS, ischial tuberosity, pubic tubercle and acetabulum.

🦴

Femur

The longest and strongest bone of the body. Proximal landmarks include head, neck, greater and lesser trochanters. Distally it has medial and lateral condyles and epicondyles.

🦴

Patella

A sesamoid bone within the quadriceps tendon. It improves the mechanical advantage of the extensor mechanism and protects the anterior knee.

🦴

Tibia

The major weight-bearing bone of the leg. Key landmarks include tibial tuberosity, medial condyle, anterior border and medial malleolus.

🦴

Fibula

A slender lateral leg bone. It contributes to ankle stability and provides important muscle attachments; its distal end forms the lateral malleolus.

🦶

Foot Bones

The foot contains seven tarsals, five metatarsals and phalanges. The calcaneus and talus are especially important for ankle and hindfoot mechanics.

03

Joints of the Lower Limb

Sacroiliac Joint

Connects sacrum with ilium and transfers load between the spine and lower limbs. It has limited but important movement.

Hip Joint

A synovial ball-and-socket joint between femoral head and acetabulum. It provides mobility while maintaining stability for weight-bearing.

Knee Joint

A complex synovial joint involving tibiofemoral and patellofemoral articulations. Menisci, ligaments and muscles contribute to stability.

Tibiofibular Joints

The proximal tibiofibular joint permits small gliding movements; the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis helps maintain the ankle mortise.

Ankle Joint

The talocrural joint mainly permits dorsiflexion and plantarflexion. The malleoli form a stable mortise around the talus.

Foot Joints

Subtalar and intertarsal joints contribute to foot adaptation, while metatarsophalangeal joints are important during push-off.

Knee stability: Stability comes from the shape of the joint surfaces, capsule, ligaments, menisci and dynamic control from surrounding muscles.
04

Muscles — Attachments, Actions & Nerve Supply

Gluteus Maximus

Origin: posterior ilium, sacrum and related fascia. Insertion: iliotibial tract and gluteal tuberosity. Action: powerful hip extension and assists external rotation. Nerve: inferior gluteal.

Gluteus Medius

From lateral ilium to greater trochanter. Abducts the hip and assists pelvic stability during single-leg stance. Superior gluteal nerve.

Gluteus Minimus

Deep gluteal muscle inserting on the greater trochanter region. Abducts and assists medial rotation of the hip. Superior gluteal nerve.

Iliopsoas

Major hip flexor formed by iliacus and psoas major. Inserts on the lesser trochanter and is important during hip flexion and gait.

Quadriceps Femoris

Rectus femoris plus vastus lateralis, medialis and intermedius. Extends the knee; rectus femoris also flexes the hip. Femoral nerve.

Sartorius

Runs from ASIS toward the medial proximal tibia. Flexes, abducts and laterally rotates the hip and flexes the knee. Femoral nerve.

Hamstrings

Semitendinosus, semimembranosus and biceps femoris. Generally extend the hip and flex the knee. Mostly supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve; short head of biceps by common fibular division.

Adductor Group

Adductor longus, brevis, magnus and related muscles contribute to thigh adduction. Most are supplied by the obturator nerve, with important exceptions.

Tibialis Anterior

Anterior leg muscle inserting mainly on the medial foot. Dorsiflexes and inverts the foot. Deep fibular nerve.

Gastrocnemius

Crosses knee and ankle. Powerful plantarflexor and assists knee flexion. Tibial nerve.

Soleus

Deep calf muscle important for sustained plantarflexion and postural control. Tibial nerve.

Fibularis Longus

Lateral compartment muscle. Everts the foot and assists plantarflexion; tendon crosses beneath the foot to insert medially. Superficial fibular nerve.

Functional memory: Anterior leg muscles mainly dorsiflex; lateral compartment mainly everts; posterior compartment mainly plantarflexes.
05

Nerves & Major Peripheral Pathways

Lumbar & Sacral Plexuses

Lower-limb innervation arises mainly from the lumbar and sacral plexuses. Their branches supply the hip, thigh, leg and foot.

Femoral Nerve

Major nerve of the anterior thigh. It supplies quadriceps and provides sensory innervation through branches including the saphenous nerve.

Obturator Nerve

Main nerve of the medial thigh compartment and a key nerve for hip adduction.

Sciatic Nerve

Large nerve of the posterior thigh and lower limb. It divides into tibial and common fibular components, usually in the popliteal region or higher.

Tibial Nerve

Supplies the posterior leg and many intrinsic foot muscles. It continues behind the medial malleolus toward the plantar foot.

Common Fibular Nerve

Wraps around the neck of the fibula and divides into superficial and deep fibular nerves. It is vulnerable at the fibular neck.

06

Blood Supply

External Iliac → Femoral

The external iliac artery becomes the femoral artery after passing beneath the inguinal ligament. The femoral artery supplies the anterior thigh and continues toward the knee.

Deep Femoral Artery

The profunda femoris is a major deep branch supplying the thigh through perforating and circumflex branches.

Popliteal Artery

The femoral artery becomes the popliteal artery behind the knee. It then divides into major branches supplying the leg and foot.

Anterior & Posterior Tibial

These arteries distribute blood to the leg and foot. The posterior tibial artery passes behind the medial malleolus and contributes to plantar circulation.

Dorsalis Pedis

The dorsalis pedis artery is the continuation of the anterior tibial artery on the dorsum of the foot and is an important clinical pulse point.

Venous Return

Superficial veins include great and small saphenous veins. Deep veins accompany arteries and drain toward the femoral and pelvic venous systems.

07

Regional Anatomy

Gluteal Region

Contains major gluteal muscles, short lateral rotators, the sciatic nerve and important vessels and nerves passing between pelvic structures.

Femoral Triangle

A clinically important region in the upper anterior thigh containing major vascular and neural structures.

Adductor Canal

A passage through the thigh transmitting femoral vessels toward the popliteal region and carrying the saphenous nerve.

Popliteal Fossa

A diamond-shaped space behind the knee containing important vessels and nerves, including the popliteal artery and vein and tibial/common fibular nerves.

Tarsal Tunnel

A passage behind the medial malleolus containing the tibial nerve and tendons/vessels. Compression can produce plantar sensory symptoms.

Foot Arches

Medial, lateral and transverse arches help distribute load, absorb shock and support efficient propulsion during gait.

09

Clinical Anatomy for Physiotherapy

Knee Injuries

Assessment may involve pain, swelling, range of motion, strength, stability and functional tasks. Ligament, meniscal and patellofemoral problems require different clinical reasoning.

ACL Relevance

The anterior cruciate ligament contributes to knee stability. Rehabilitation commonly considers quadriceps strength, neuromuscular control, movement quality and progressive loading.

Hip Osteoarthritis

May affect pain, hip range, strength and walking. Functional assessment helps determine limitations and guide rehabilitation.

Ankle Sprain

Lateral ankle sprains commonly involve the lateral ligament complex. Rehabilitation considers swelling, range, strength, balance and progressive return to activity.

Foot Drop

Weakness of ankle dorsiflexion can produce foot-drop gait. Common fibular/deep fibular nerve dysfunction is one possible neurological cause; full assessment is needed.

Plantar Fascia Pain

Heel/plantar pain can be influenced by load, tissue capacity and foot mechanics. Assessment should consider the whole kinetic chain.

Important: This page is for education and revision. Diagnosis and treatment decisions require appropriate clinical examination, professional training and current clinical guidance.

High-Yield Revision Notes

Bone Sequence

Hip bone → femur → patella → tibia/fibula → tarsals → metatarsals → phalanges.

Thigh Compartments

Anterior mainly extends knee; medial mainly adducts hip; posterior mainly extends hip and flexes knee.

Leg Compartments

Anterior = dorsiflexion; lateral = eversion; posterior = plantarflexion.

Major Nerves

Femoral • obturator • sciatic • tibial • common fibular • superficial/deep fibular.

Study Every Muscle

Use: origin → insertion → action → nerve supply → functional role.

Gait Connection

Link hip, knee and ankle muscle actions to stance, swing, loading response and push-off.
